# Data Story
Pipeline step: 5 of 6 — ambursaData
Date: 16 November 2026
Business question: How are people in Exeter using AI, and where does Exeter differ from the UK picture?
Audience: Mixed: local organisations (University of Exeter, Exeter Students' Guild, Exeter City and Devon County councils, business groups) and a general/LinkedIn audience
Prepared by: ambursaData

---

## Executive Summary
Exeter is probably one of the UK's heavier AI-using places: an estimated two in three adults (63–67%) have used tools like ChatGPT, against six in ten nationally, mainly because the city is unusually young and full of students. But that average hides two groups who are being left behind. The first is roughly 20,000 older residents who mostly don't use AI. The second is students who use it every day but haven't been taught to use it well: four in five have caught it getting facts wrong, and only about half got helpful guidance even in the best-served faculty. Students who avoid AI mostly do so on principle, above all because of its environmental cost, rather than through lack of skill. The most important action is to shift support from rules about AI towards teaching people to use and check it, starting with student workshops (wanted by 55%) and an AI-confidence offer for older residents.

---

## Part 1 — The Big Picture
In under four years, AI chatbots have gone from novelty to everyday tool. Across the UK, 59% of adults used generative AI in the three months to March 2026, the first time an official survey has measured it on this scale. This report asks what that looks like in Exeter: who is using AI, what for, what holds people back, and where Exeter differs from the national picture.

There's one honest limit up front: **nobody has yet surveyed Exeter residents as a whole about AI.** What does exist is solid national data, census data on who lives in Exeter, a February 2026 survey of University of Exeter students by the Students' Guild, regional business surveys, and public records of how local organisations use AI. We've put these 12 sources together and, where Exeter-specific numbers don't exist, estimated them openly. Student figures come from a volunteer panel, so treat them as a strong signal rather than an exact count.

---

## Part 2 — What the Data Reveals

### Exeter is probably ahead of the UK on AI use, but its non-users are concentrated among older residents
Age is the single best predictor of whether someone uses AI. Nationally, about six in ten people in their twenties use it, while nine in ten people over 65 don't. Exeter is unusually young: **40% of its adults are aged 16–34, against 30% across England**, and one in six residents aged 16+ is a full-time student, roughly three times the national share.

When we apply national usage rates to Exeter's population, two different methods both land above the national figure: **an estimated 63–67% of Exeter adults have used generative AI, against 59% for the UK.** That's roughly 74,000–79,000 local adults. It also means around 40,000 haven't, and about half of those are aged 65 or over.

*On the dashboard:* "Two in five Exeter adults are aged 16–34" compares the age profiles, and "Every estimation method puts Exeter above the UK's measured rate" shows each estimate beside the national figure.

### Exeter students use AI as a tutor, not a ghostwriter, and trust and support haven't caught up
Among University of Exeter students, AI use is close to universal: only 8% have never used it for their studies. The most common use is **understanding a difficult concept (71%)**, about ten points higher than UK undergraduates report for similar tasks. Exeter students are a little *less* likely than the national average to use AI to summarise readings. ChatGPT dominates (84%), and no other tool reaches even half as many students. That includes the University's own licensed Copilot (35%).

The weak spot is trust. **79% have noticed AI giving them false information**, and 37% worry fairly or very often about being wrongly accused of misusing it. Even in the faculty with the best guidance, only half said they had received helpful advice. Asked what would help, students chose **workshops and training (55%)** far ahead of banning AI (10%).

*On the dashboard:* "Exeter students use AI to understand concepts more often than UK undergraduates do", "ChatGPT reaches more than twice as many Exeter students as any other tool", and the two trust and support charts.

### Exeter's AI refusers object on principle, not because they lack skills
Nationally, people who don't use AI mostly say they'd simply rather not (44%), or worry about privacy (33%) or not knowing enough (32%). Exeter students who avoid AI give a very different answer: **58% cite its environmental impact**, followed by wanting to think for themselves (20%) and ethical concerns (11%). In humanities and social sciences, three in four students agree AI risks replacing critical thinking.

This is a small group, and the national survey didn't offer "environmental impact" as an answer, so the contrast is indicative. But the direction is clear: Exeter's non-users have made a values-based choice.

*On the dashboard:* the two side-by-side charts in section 3.

## Part 3 — Why It Matters
"Exeter is AI-savvy" and "Exeter has a large group left out of AI" are both true. A general campaign aimed at "the public" would mostly reach people who already use AI and miss the roughly 20,000 older residents who don't. As public services move online and introduce AI (Royal Devon is already using an AI scribe in thousands of consultations, and Devon County Council has rolled out Copilot to staff), people who don't understand AI are more likely to be confused by it, wary of it, or exposed to scams that use it.

For students, the risk is quality rather than access. Students use AI daily, mostly self-taught, while catching it getting things wrong. Without training, mistakes end up in coursework and wrongful-misconduct worries grow, and graduates leave without the "use it well and check it" skill employers now expect. The fact that students mainly use a tool the University doesn't license (ChatGPT, 84%) rather than its approved one (Copilot, 35%) is also a data-protection and consistency issue the University can't manage through policy alone.

## Part 4 — The Hidden Opportunity
**CONFIRMED:** most students taught themselves to use AI. 60% say they had no introduction to AI in any learning setting (67% of UK-domiciled students), yet nearly all use it. They are asking, in their own words, for workshops.
**OPPORTUNITY:** demand is already there and doesn't need creating. A short, practical "check what the AI tells you" workshop, built around the 79% who've been misled, meets a stated need. The same format could be adapted for older residents and small businesses, so one programme could serve all three groups.

**HYPOTHESIS:** environmental concern could be an asset, not an obstacle. Exeter is a climate-research city (the Met Office is headquartered here, and the University has a strong climate profile), and its AI refusers care about AI's footprint. This suggests "lower-impact, use-it-when-it-helps" guidance would land better in Exeter than a "use AI more" message. To test this, run one workshop with an AI-and-environment framing and compare sign-ups.

**HYPOTHESIS:** South West small firms are behind. 28% of South West SMEs don't use AI at all, against 19% UK-wide and 7% in London. If Exeter firms mirror the region, there's room for local business-support providers to close a measurable gap. That would need an Exeter business survey to confirm.

---

## Part 5 — What We Should Do
1. **Run practical AI-verification workshops for students this academic year.** 55% ask for them and 79% have been misled by AI. Owner: University of Exeter digital skills team with the Guild (or an external trainer such as AmbursaTech). Timeframe: pilot in Term 2 (Jan–Mar 2027).
2. **Launch an "AI confidence" session for older residents.** About 20,000 Exeter adults aged 65+ are likely non-users. Owner: Age UK Exeter and Exeter libraries, with Exeter City Council. Timeframe: pilot by spring 2027.
3. **Close the gap between licensed and actually used tools.** Copilot reaches 35% of students against 84% for ChatGPT. Either promote the approved tools or publish guidance on safe use of consumer tools. Owner: University digital services. Timeframe: before the 2027/28 academic year.
4. **Measure Exeter directly.** Add 3–4 AI questions to Exeter City Council's annual residents survey (1,100 residents), so next year's figures are measured rather than estimated. Owner: Exeter City Council insight team. Timeframe: summer 2027 survey round.

Open questions for investigation:
→ What does the official South West regional figure (DSIT table E6) show, and does it support the 63–67% estimate?
→ How do Exeter's non-student, non-graduate and older residents actually use AI?
→ How many Exeter businesses use AI, and for what?

## Part 6 — The Potential Impact
If these actions are taken, the most likely outcomes are:
→ **Better student AI skills and less anxiety:** workshops target the 79% who've been misled and the 37% who worry about being wrongly flagged. Success means fewer students reporting misinformation problems and misconduct worries in the Guild's next AI survey.
→ **More older residents confident with AI:** reaching even 10% of the roughly 20,000 likely non-users aged 65+ would mean about 2,000 more people confident with AI. *Calculation: 23,100 Exeter adults aged 65+ × 89% non-use (2025 national rate) ≈ 20,600; 10% ≈ 2,000. This is illustrative and an upper bound, since older adults' use has risen since 2025.*
→ **Measured, not modelled, local evidence:** adding AI questions to the residents survey would replace this report's main estimate with a real figure within a year.

The biggest risk of doing nothing is that Exeter's average looks healthy while support keeps going to people who already use AI, and the older and less confident residents the average hides fall further behind.

## Quality Note
- The Exeter-wide figure (63–67%) is **modelled** from national usage by age and Exeter's student share. It isn't measured, and it may be an underestimate because it doesn't account for Exeter's many graduates.
- Exeter usage figures describe **University of Exeter students only**, from a self-selecting Guild panel whose response count isn't published.
- Comparisons between surveys are **directional**: questions, wording and dates differ.
- Business figures from different sources use **different definitions** and shouldn't be compared directly. None are Exeter-only.
