## Executive Summary
An estimated two in three Exeter adults (63–67%) have used generative AI, against 59% nationally, because the city is unusually young and full of students. That matters because the healthy average hides roughly 20,000 older residents who mostly don't use AI, and students who use it daily but were never taught to check it: 79% have caught it giving false information. The data also shows the demand is already there: 55% of students are asking for workshops, and only 10% want AI banned. The most important action is to pilot practical "use it and check it" AI workshops for students in Term 2 (Jan–Mar 2027), then adapt the same format for older residents. If both pilots run by spring 2027 and AI questions are added to the city's residents survey, Exeter will have measured local figures and a tested support model within a year.

---

## Key Findings Recap
• An estimated 63–67% of Exeter adults have used generative AI, against 59% for the UK; about half of the city's ~40,000 non-users are aged 65+.
• 71% of Exeter students use AI to understand concepts, but 79% have seen it give false information and only half got helpful guidance even in the best-served faculty.
• Exeter students who avoid AI mostly do so because of its environmental impact (58%), unlike UK non-users, who mostly say they'd rather not (44%).

## Business Impact Assessment

### Finding 1: Exeter is ahead on average, but older residents are left out
**Current state:** An estimated 63–67% adult use (modelled). About 23,100 Exeter adults are aged 65+, and nationally 89% of that age group didn't use AI in 2025.
**Trend:** Use is rising across all age groups nationally, and fastest among older adults, though from a low starting point. Exeter-specific trend: unknown (no local time series).
**Impact if unchanged:**
  → Service cost: digital-first and AI-assisted services (e.g. Royal Devon's AI scribe, Devon County Council's Copilot) may face more confusion, complaints and hand-holding from residents unfamiliar with AI. Scale not measurable from this data.
  → Residents: up to ~20,600 older adults remain non-users. *Calculation: 23,100 × 89% = 20,559, using 2025 national rates. This is an upper bound.*
  → Risk: exposure to AI-enabled scams and misinformation is higher among people who don't know what AI can do. This is a direction, not a quantified risk.
**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Two independent estimates agree on direction, but the figures are modelled.

### Finding 2: Students use AI widely, but trust and support lag
**Current state:** 92% have used AI for study; 79% have seen false information; 37% worry often about being wrongly flagged; 50% got helpful guidance in the best faculty.
**Trend:** UK undergraduate use rose from 66% (2024) to 95% (2026); including AI-generated text in assessed work rose from 3% to 12%.
**Impact if unchanged:**
  → Academic quality: AI errors carried into coursework. Not quantifiable here.
  → Cost: staff time spent on suspected-misconduct cases is likely to grow as AI-text inclusion rises. This is a direction only.
  → Employability: graduates leave without structured AI-verification skills. 60% of students say AI skills are an important part of their education.
  → Risk: data protection. 84% use a consumer tool (ChatGPT) against 35% for the licensed Copilot.
**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The Guild panel is self-selecting, but its direction matches national HEPI data.

### Finding 3: Non-use is values-based
**Current state:** Among Exeter student non-users: environment 58%, self-reliance 20%, ethics 11%. In HASS, 75% agree AI risks replacing critical thinking.
**Trend:** Unknown (single wave).
**Impact if unchanged:**
  → Engagement: generic "how to use AI" offers won't reach this group. Scale unknown, because the non-user base isn't published.
  → Risk: a divide between AI users and principled refusers within the same courses.
**Confidence:** LOW–MEDIUM. The base is small and the two surveys used different answer lists.

## Strategic Opportunities

### Opportunity 1: One "use it and check it" programme for three audiences
**What the data shows:** 55% of students want workshops; 79% have been misled by AI; 60% had no introduction to AI in a learning setting.
**What this means:** The demand is stated, not assumed. A short, practical format about checking AI output works for students, and could be adapted for older residents and small firms.
**What would need to be true:** Students turn up when workshops are offered (stated demand becomes attendance), and the content transfers to non-student audiences.
**How to test it:** Run 2–3 student pilot workshops in Term 2 and measure sign-ups, attendance and before/after confidence.
**Potential impact:** Direction: better verification habits and less anxiety about misconduct. Scale depends on reach; the panel's n isn't published, so student numbers can't be sized.
**Decision needed:** The University and Guild agree who hosts the pilot and fund one term's sessions.

### Opportunity 2: An AI-confidence offer for older residents
**What the data shows:** About 23,100 Exeter adults aged 65+, with 89% national non-use in 2025. Age UK Exeter already has an AI-in-the-workplace policy, so the organisation is already engaged with AI.
**What this means:** A large, identifiable group with an existing trusted channel (Age UK Exeter, libraries).
**What would need to be true:** Older residents want this (not tested), and partners have capacity.
**How to test it:** One pilot session through Age UK Exeter or a library, collecting interest sign-ups.
**Potential impact:** Reaching 10% of likely non-users ≈ 2,000 people (illustrative upper bound; see Finding 1 calculation).
**Decision needed:** Age UK Exeter, Exeter libraries and the City Council agree a pilot partner.

### Opportunity 3: Lead with low-impact guidance in a climate-conscious city (hypothesis)
**What the data shows:** 58% of student non-users cite environmental impact.
**What this means:** Framing AI guidance around "use it when it genuinely helps" may engage people a "use AI more" message would push away.
**What would need to be true:** Environmental concern is common beyond the non-user subset.
**How to test it:** A/B test workshop invitations, one standard and one environment-framed, and compare sign-up rates.
**Potential impact:** Unknown until tested.
**Decision needed:** Whoever runs Opportunity 1 includes the A/B test.
